## Step 1. Ask

- **Alcohol (29 Oct).** Alcohol is probably what sits behind many heart disease and accident deaths, so I want it in the data. I take it in two stages. First, alcohol-related causes of death (alcohol abuse, chronic liver disease) from the deaths table I already plan to download, `hlth_cd_asdr2`, so no new source. Second, only if time allows after the five tables are joined in Step 4: alcohol consumption per person from the WHO Global Health Observatory as a sixth table. Eurostat's own drinking survey (EHIS) only covers a few years, so I don't use it. Alcohol overlaps with heart disease and accidents, so in Exploration I check which factor still matters.

## Step 2. Gathering data

- **Raw means raw (29 Oct).** The only selection is in the download link (sex, age, years), and anyone can read it there. After the download I change nothing in Section 2. Even `clean_names()` waits until Section 3, so the raw tables keep Eurostat's own column names, and every change to the data happens where I can explain it.

- **All 93 causes of death, chapters first (1 Nov).** The deaths table has 93 causes. I don't pick a few at download, because choosing only the causes my hypotheses name could hide a cause I didn't think of, and that would be biased by design. So I download all 93 (women and men, under 65). In the analysis I first look at the big chapters of causes (circulatory, cancer, external causes, respiratory and so on), which don't overlap and together cover all deaths, without choosing anything. That overview can prove my expected message wrong. Only then I look deeper into the specific causes my hypotheses name (ischaemic heart disease, suicide, alcohol-related causes). Causes that turn out not to matter go into the dead ends, not into the bin. Some causes sit inside others (ischaemic heart disease is part of all circulatory diseases), so they are compared, never added up.
- **Deaths table: the 27 EU countries are chosen in the link (1 Nov).** For the first two tables I removed the non-EU countries in cleaning. The deaths table is different: besides 36 countries it has about 460 regions, and the full file would be 55 MB with 685 637 rows. I don't download data I don't need, so the link asks only for the 27 member states: 3.5 MB, 43 902 rows. The selection is still visible in the link, like the choice of sex and age.

## Step 3. Cleaning

- **Flagged values are kept (30 Oct).** Some life expectancy values carry a letter, for example `e` (estimated), `p` (provisional) or `b` (break in the series). The letter says how the number was made, not that it is wrong, so I keep every flagged value. In the 27 EU countries only 52 of 540 values have a flag, all in Poland, Romania, Germany, France, Luxembourg, Czechia and Italy. Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania have none.
- **Technical columns are removed (30 Oct).** Six columns describe the download, not the data: `dataflow`, `last_update`, `freq`, `unit`, `age` and `conf_status`. The checks showed that `freq`, `unit` and `age` hold one value each (the choices I made in the link) and that `conf_status` is empty, so they tell me nothing new. Keeping them would also give the joined table in Section 4 many repeated columns with clashing names. The one useful fact, the `last_update` date, goes into the source line once instead of sitting in every row. I keep five columns: `country_code`, `sex`, `year`, `life_expectancy` and `flag`.
- **The flag column stays, for now (30 Oct).** If a value looks odd in Exploration, the flag tells me straight away whether it was estimated, provisional or a break in the series, without going back to the raw file. It's one small column, so it costs almost nothing now. I decide in Section 4 whether it goes into `gap_panel`, because that's where it starts to cost something: `pivot_wider()` would split it into one flag for women and one for men. Dropping it later is one line, `select(-flag)`. Getting it back after dropping it would mean redoing 3.1.
- **Luxembourg stays, with two years missing (31 Oct).** Luxembourg has no healthy life years for 2023 and 2024. I keep its 16 real values and leave the two years missing, nothing filled in. Dropping Luxembourg would throw away 8 good years to hide 2 missing ones, and cutting everyone to 2015 to 2022 would cost me the latest Baltic figures because of one small country. In charts for 2023 and 2024 Luxembourg simply has no healthy life years dot, and I name the gap in Limits.
- **Germany 2022 marked low reliability (31 Oct).** Germany's healthy life years for 2022 carry the flag `bu`: a break in the series and low reliability. I keep the values like all other flagged ones, but if Germany jumps in 2022 in Exploration, I'll check this flag first.
- **One code for "all causes" (1 Nov).** In the deaths table, Eurostat calls all causes `TOTAL` in some years and `A-R_V-Y` in 2018 to 2021. Same number, two names, and never both in the same year. I rename `A-R_V-Y` to `TOTAL`, so all causes has one code in all nine years. Otherwise every filter later would have to remember both codes, and forgetting it once would quietly lose four years. No row is lost, nothing is counted twice.
- **A column that marks the level of each cause (1 Nov).** The 93 causes sit at three levels: all causes, 17 chapters (plus COVID-19 from 2020), and detailed causes inside the chapters. I checked that the chapters plus COVID-19 add up to all causes in every country, sex and year, so the chapter view really covers every death. I add a column `cause_level` with "all causes", "chapter" or "detail" and keep all rows. Then the unbiased chapter overview and the deeper look at my hypothesis causes are each one `filter()` away. Splitting into two tables would be harder to keep in line, and waiting until Exploration would hide the chapter list inside the charts.
- **Education: no new decision, one thing to remember (1 Nov).** The education table needed no new decision, everything was covered by earlier ones: keep flagged values, keep the EU, drop the technical columns. One thing to remember for Exploration: 72 of the 104 `b` flags are in 2021, when the EU changed the method of its labour force survey. A jump between 2020 and 2021 might be the new method, not a real change.
- **Luxembourg and Ireland stay in the GDP table (1 Nov).** Their GDP index is far above everyone else's: Luxembourg because many of its workers live across the border, Ireland because big international companies book their profits there. So GDP makes them look richer than the people who live there are. The numbers are still real Eurostat data, so I don't change or remove them. If I removed the two countries, I'd lose them from every other comparison too, where they're perfectly normal. In Exploration I'll check every chart against GDP with and without them, and show both if the line moves.
- **Zeros in the deaths table are kept (1 Nov).** 3 151 death rates are exactly 0: no deaths from that cause under 65 in that country and year. That's a real count, not a missing value.
- **Charts and neat tables in the checks (30 Oct).** I show the check results as charts and tables instead of console text, so the report is easier to read. A `tbl_summary()` table for missing values, histograms split by sex for the range, and a bar chart of rows per country. Small tables go through `knitr::kable()`, which only changes how they look. The check charts use colour from the viridis family, the course's default palette, which colour-blind readers can also tell apart. Each chart has its own pair of colours, so one colour never means two things: blue and green for women and men, purple and orange for other units and EU countries. No red, because red is kept for the Baltic states in the whole project.
- **EU filter in cleaning, not in combining (30 Oct).** I remove the non-EU countries and the EU totals in Section 3, table by table, right after the checks. That way each clean table holds only what the analysis uses, and Section 4 can focus on reshaping and joining. The years don't need a filter at all, because the download links already ask for 2015 to 2024.

## Step 4. Combining the tables

- **One table, one row per country and year (1 Nov).** I join the five tables into `gap_panel`: 27 countries times 10 years, 270 rows. My questions are about relationships, like whether a bigger gap goes with lower GDP, and a chart can only show that when both numbers are in the same row. Life expectancy is complete, so it is the backbone that everything else is joined onto. Anything a table does not have stays `NA`, never 0.
- **Flags are dropped before reshaping (1 Nov).** When women and men go side by side, `flag` would split into two columns for every table, so about 8 flag columns in the panel. The flags are already counted and described table by table in Section 3, so nothing is lost if I drop them here.
- **Two tables, not one (1 Nov).** The deaths data has 92 causes. Putting all of them into `gap_panel` would add about 180 columns, which nobody could read and which breaks the rule of one thing per column. So `gap_panel` gets only deaths from all causes, and a second table, `deaths_by_cause`, keeps one row per country, year and cause, with women and men side by side. That second table is the one I use for the chapter overview and the deeper look at my hypothesis causes.
- **The deaths gap is men minus women (1 Nov).** For deaths, men are the ones with the higher number, so I subtract the other way round: men minus women, in deaths per 100 000. Then a positive number means "men worse off", like a positive life expectancy gap. I chose a difference, not a ratio, because the ratio breaks when women have 0 deaths from a cause (3 151 times in the data, and dividing by 0 gives `Inf`). Both rates stay in the table, so I can still say "men under 65 die about 3 times as often" in the talk (Latvia 2023: 601.6 against 197.3).
- **`left_join()` from life expectancy (1 Nov).** Life expectancy is my backbone: every country and year that has a gap stays in the panel, 270 rows. `inner_join()` would silently drop every row that is missing in any table, so all of 2024 (the deaths end in 2023) and Luxembourg 2023, 242 rows left, and I would lose my newest year in every chart. With `left_join()` the missing values show up as `NA`, and `anti_join()` lists exactly which ones.
- **Country names (1 Nov).** The panel only had codes like `LV`. Most people know `DE`, fewer know `EL` (Greece) or `SI` (Slovenia), so I add a column with the English names, written once in a small table. Charts, the app and the talk then say "Latvia".

## Step 5. Exploration

- **Which specific causes to look at (2 Nov).** After the chapter overview, I go deeper only where it points: the three largest chapters, circulatory, external and digestive. Inside them I take every specific cause at one level, so nothing is hand-picked and nothing is counted twice (the causes add up exactly to their chapter). I add alcohol disorders (`F10`) and lung cancer because my hypotheses name them. Choosing the causes that "look interesting" would be biased, especially since the numbers were already looked at once to check the data. The rule decides, not the result.
- **One graph per factor (2 Nov).** I first had all five factors as five panels in one chart. That was too much at once, so I look at them one by one: one chart, one factor, one question, with its correlation right below. It also means five separate graphs for the template. And one caution I want to remember: the death factors are partly built into the gap, because life expectancy is calculated from death rates. A strong correlation there is expected. GDP and education are the real tests.
- **Education is a dead end (2 Nov).** Across all 27 countries it looks moderate (0.52), but without the Baltic states it is only 0.22. Slovenia has the same education gap as Latvia and a much smaller life expectancy gap. So hypothesis 5 doesn't hold across Europe. It stays in the report as a dead end, because a weak result is also a result.
- **"Only wealth?" answered inside 5.4 (2 Nov).** I had planned a separate step to check whether the death factors only stand in for wealth. Graph 8 already answers it: the Baltic gaps are about two years larger than their income would predict, and Latvia and Greece are equally rich with very different gaps. So I fold that step into 5.4 and go straight to the Rosling chart.
- **Graph 10: compare with equally rich countries (2 Nov).** The Rosling chart (54 dots, 27 lines) and then a chart of "years above the pattern line" were both too hard to read. The question is simple, so the answer should be too: I compare the Baltic states with the ten other EU countries of similar income (GDP index 60 to 90, the range the Baltic states are in). Baltic women live as long as women there (82.1 against 82.3 years), Baltic men almost 3 years shorter (73.2 against 76.0). The Rosling chart may still come back in the story, built up in frames.
