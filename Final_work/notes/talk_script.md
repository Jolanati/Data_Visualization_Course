# Talk script: 3 minutes, one graph

**Setup before you start:** open `figures/final_graph.png` full screen (double-click it, then F11 or the full-screen button). Nothing else on the screen. The teacher stops the clock at 3:00. Sergio's rule is one graph only, so the whole talk stays on this one image.

About 380 spoken words, close to 3 minutes at a calm pace. If a rehearsal runs over 2:50, drop the line marked *(optional)*.

---

## Part 1 · 0:00 to 0:30 · The question and the finding

**Show:** the whole graph. Don't point yet.

**Say:**

> In every EU country, women live longer than men. But in Latvia, where I live, the gap is almost ten years, nearly twice the EU average.
>
> So my question was: how much shorter do Baltic men live than Baltic women, compared with the rest of the EU, and why?
>
> My answer: at the same income, Baltic women live as long as other Europeans, but Baltic men live up to 4.6 years shorter.

---

## Part 2 · 0:30 to 1:45 · Reading the graph

**Show:** point to the **subtitle** under the title.

**Say:**

> Each bar compares a Baltic country with ten EU countries that are about as rich, like Poland, Hungary or Croatia. That takes income out of the picture.
>
> A bar at zero means the same as in equally rich countries. To the left means shorter lives.

**Show:** point to the **left panel, the gray bars (Women)**.

**Say:**

> On the left, the women. All three countries are within about one year of zero. Baltic women live about as long as women in comparable countries.

**Show:** point to the **right panel, the red bars (Men)**, from the bottom (Estonia) up to the top (Latvia).

**Say:**

> On the right, the men. Every bar goes to the left. Estonia almost one year, Lithuania three years, and Latvia 4.6 years shorter.

**Show:** point to the **red text next to Latvia**, the annotation.

**Say:**

> Latvian men live 71.4 years. Men in equally rich countries live 76.
>
> So the Baltic gap is not about being poorer. It's about the men.

---

## Part 3 · 1:45 to 2:30 · Why the men, and how I got there

**Show:** point to the **source line at the bottom** while you mention the data, then back to the **red bars**.

**Say:**

> I worked with six data tables, from Eurostat and the WHO, for all 27 EU countries from 2015 to 2024.
>
> Before I opened the data, I guessed heart disease. That was right, but only half the story. Baltic men under 65 also die far more often from external causes: accidental poisoning is six times the EU level, and suicide and drowning are much higher too. Several of these point to alcohol.
>
> *(optional)* My first draft showed total life expectancy as bars from zero to eighty, and the difference was almost invisible. Showing the difference instead is what made the finding visible.

---

## Part 4 · 2:30 to 3:00 · Limit and close

**Show:** the **whole graph** again. Stop pointing.

**Say:**

> One limit: these are country averages. They show what goes together, not what causes it.
>
> But the picture is clear. At the same income, Baltic women keep up with Europe. Baltic men don't, and in Latvia they live 4.6 years shorter.
>
> Thank you.

---

## If Sergio asks a question afterwards

| Likely question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Why compare with countries of similar income? | The Baltic states are poorer than most of the EU. Comparing with the EU average would mix up income and health. |
| Which countries are "similar income"? | The ten EU countries with a GDP per person of 60 to 90% of the EU average: Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Greece, Hungary, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia. |
| Does the result depend on that choice? | No. I tried three other income bands. The numbers move, but the men always fall 2 to 4 years further behind than the women. |
| Why gray and red? | Gray for the women, one colour for the men, the message. Red is much darker than gray, so it also works for colour blind people. I checked it in a simulator. |
| Why bars and not points? | One number per country and sex, a difference from zero. A bar's length shows the size of the difference directly. |
| Is it alcohol? | The deaths point to alcohol, but the data shows what goes together, not the cause. Alcohol data per person isn't split by sex. |
